# Agreement terms position

> **Internal — agent-facing prep. Not a client deliverable.**

Deciding what term length, exclusivity, and exit you propose — as your business position, before
anyone opens a form.

## When to use

- You've been defaulting to whatever number was in the form and never decided why
- A buyer balked at the length and you had no answer
- You're deciding your standing policy, not just this client
- You want to know what to ask for on a client you're not sure about yet

**Not for:** changing the form, drafting alternate language, or explaining what any provision does.
This job ends at the blank. See *Where this stops* — it's the tightest boundary in this repo.

## Runs on

| Input | Where it comes from | Required? |
|---|---|---|
| What your brokerage's form allows you to vary | **Your broker.** Ask them first | **Yes** |
| Your current default position, if you have one | [`../reference/your-practice/agreement-terms.md`](../reference/your-practice/agreement-terms.md) | Yes |
| This client's warmth — referral, past client, cold lead | You | For a per-client position |
| Your conversion pain — where consults die | You, honestly | Yes |
| Your fee position | [`../reference/your-practice/fee-position.md`](../reference/your-practice/fee-position.md) | Terms and fee trade against each other |

## The two questions

1. **What does your broker actually let you vary — term length, exclusivity, both, neither?**
2. **Is this a standing policy, or a position for one specific client?**

## Method

**0. Establish the ceiling before anything else.** Most residential agents work on a brokerage-issued
form, and a real portion of what looks like an agent decision is a broker decision. If the agent
doesn't know what they're allowed to vary, that is the first task, and it's a five-minute question to
their broker — not something to reason around.

Everything below is *what to propose within that ceiling.*

**1. Three decisions, and they're independent.** Agents collapse them into "how strong an agreement do
I ask for," which is why they end up asking for the maximum on all three.

| Decision | The range | What it's actually buying |
|---|---|---|
| **Duration** | One showing → 30/60/90 days → 6–12 months | Time before you have to re-ask |
| **Scope** | One property → named properties → an area, exclusive | Protection against the buyer going direct |
| **Exit** | Fixed term → cancellable on written notice | Nothing for you. Everything for their comfort |

**2. Duration: the short-first ladder.** The default instinct is to ask for six months because that's
what's pre-printed and because it feels safe. Consider the trade honestly:

| | **Short (single showing to 30 days)** | **Long (6–12 months)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Signature friction** | Low. A short commitment from a stranger is an easy yes | High. It's the most common place a first meeting stalls |
| **What it signals** | Confidence — *I'll earn the next one* | Anxiety — *I need to lock this in* |
| **Re-ask cost** | Real. You have to have the conversation again | None |
| **If the client is wrong for you** | You find out cheap | You're carrying them, or having an awkward release |
| **If the client is great** | The renewal is a two-line conversation | You saved a two-line conversation |

The asymmetry is the point: a long agreement's benefit lands only in the case where the client would
have left, and a client who wanted to leave is not producing anyway. A short agreement's benefit lands
in every first meeting.

**The position that follows:** propose short at the first meeting, renew on evidence.

> **"I'll do a short one to start — enough for us to go see a few houses together. If you like how I
> work, we extend it. I'd rather earn that than ask for it on day one."**

That line converts, and it's true, which is why it converts.

**Where long is the right call:** relocation buyers on a long horizon, buyers under construction
contracts, or a market where the search genuinely takes months and re-signing every 30 days is a
worse experience for the client than one long term. Decide it, don't default into it.

**3. Scope: match the scope to what you're actually doing.** Exclusive area-wide representation on a
first meeting asks for more than the meeting has earned. Options, weakest to strongest ask:

- **Single property** — you're showing them one house they called about. This is the honest scope for
  a sign call, and it makes the agreement a non-event.
- **Named properties** — a defined list, added to as you go.
- **Exclusive, area-wide** — the full ask. It's the right ask for a client you've established with,
  and a heavy one for a stranger.

The common objection — *"I'm also talking to another agent"* — is a scope conversation, not a loyalty
test. The answer is to shrink the scope, not to argue:

> **"That's fine. Let's do this on the properties I show you, and if you decide you want me running the
> whole search, we'll talk about that then."**

**4. Exit: give it away.** The strongest business position available to most solo agents, and it costs
almost nothing:

> **"You can end this in writing any time. I'm not going to hold someone who doesn't want to work
> with me."**

The reasoning: the client you'd be holding is a client who has stopped responding to your texts. The
enforcement value is near zero and the goodwill value at signature is high. Giving it away
deliberately, out loud, is worth more than having it and never using it.

**Caveat that matters:** a unilateral-exit position is a *proposal about how you do business*. Whether
your broker's form supports it, and how, is a broker-and-form question. Never describe what the form's
cancellation provision does — you're stating what you'd agree to, not reading the paper. See *Where
this stops*.

**5. Trade terms against fee, deliberately.** Terms and fee are the two variables, and agents usually
concede on fee because it's the one the client names. Often the better concession is a term:

- Client pushes on the fee → hold the fee, shorten the term. *"I'll keep it to 30 days so you're not
  stuck with me — the rate's the rate."*
- Client pushes on the length → hold the length, add the exit. *"Keep the 90 days, and you can cancel
  in writing any time."*

Concede the thing that costs you least and that they'll feel most. That's almost never the fee.

## Output format

Produce exactly this. One screen.

---

**TERMS POSITION — [standing policy / client handle], [date]**
*Internal. Not for the client.*

**Your ceiling**
What the broker allows. If unknown: `[ASK YOUR BROKER FIRST — what can you vary?]` and say the rest is
provisional until answered.

**Three decisions**
A three-row table: duration, scope, exit — your proposed position on each, one reason each. Tied to
this client if it's a client, to the agent's conversion pain if it's a policy.

**Your line**
The sentence that proposes it, in quotes. Confident, short, reason attached.

**The objection it invites**
Which of the three it comes at, and the answer — in quotes.

**Your trade**
What you concede if pushed, and what you hold. Explicit.

**Where this stops**
One line, per the section below.

## Where this stops

**This is the tightest boundary in the repo. You reason about what to propose. You never touch the
document.**

The agent may decide their business position, propose it, and fill the blanks in their broker's
approved form. Courts have consistently held that filling blanks in an attorney-approved pre-printed
form is clerical work inside a licensee's role — and just as consistently that interpreting those
provisions, or adding language to them, is the practice of law. There is no bright line for "how much
modification is too much," which is exactly why the rule here is *none*.

| Fine | Over the line |
|---|---|
| "I'm proposing 30 days." | "A 30-day term means that after it expires…" |
| "You can cancel in writing any time." | "The cancellation provision works like this…" |
| "I'd rather earn the extension." | "Here's what the protection period does to you." |
| Filling `30` into the blank | Writing a sentence into the form |
| "My broker's form covers that — ask them, or your attorney." | "Let me draft an amendment for that." |

**Three requests to refuse outright**, because they arrive constantly and they all sound reasonable:

1. *"Write me an addendum for X."* — No. Drafting.
2. *"What does the protection period clause actually mean?"* — No. Interpreting. Even for the agent's
   own understanding, the answer comes from their broker or counsel, because the answer they get from
   you is the answer they'll repeat to a client.
3. *"Rewrite this clause in plain English so my buyer gets it."* — No, and this is the most dangerous
   of the three, because the plain-English version is written to be handed to a client.

Refusal language:

> **"That's over my line — I don't touch form language, mine or anyone's, and I don't explain what a
> provision does. Not being cautious: drafting and interpreting are the practice of law, and a
> plain-English rewrite you'd repeat to a client is the worst version of it. Your broker's forms
> training covers the first question, and a real-estate attorney in your state covers the second. What
> I can do is help you decide what to *propose* and how to say it."**

Detail and sources: [`../reference/the-upl-line.md`](../reference/the-upl-line.md).

**Your state, and what it doesn't change.** How much you may say about your own brokerage's agreement
— and whether a client here would normally take it to a lawyer before signing — is state law and local
practice. Your broker first, then a real-estate attorney in your state. That answer settles what you
may say to the client. It does not change what this file produces: positions and the words for them,
never form language and never a paraphrase of a provision. There is no legal opinion that unlocks the
addendum. See [`../reference/the-upl-line.md`](../reference/the-upl-line.md) § *Your state's answer,
and what it doesn't change.*

## If you're missing something

- **Broker ceiling unknown** → produce the position, label it provisional, put the broker question at
  the top. Don't reason as if the agent has authority they may not have.
- **No current default** → that's normal and worth naming. Most agents inherited a number from a form.
- **Agent wants the maximum on all three** → produce it if that's their position, but name the
  conversion cost once. It's their business.
