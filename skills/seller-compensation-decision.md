# Seller compensation decision

> **Internal — agent-facing prep. Not a client deliverable.**

Running the conversation where a seller decides whether to offer compensation to the buyer's agent —
and arriving with a recommendation instead of a shrug.

## When to use

- A listing appointment is coming and the question will be asked
- A seller has asked point-blank "do I have to pay the buyer's agent?"
- A listing has sat and you're re-opening the decision
- You've been answering "it's totally up to you" and watching sellers freeze

**Not for:** pricing the listing, writing the listing copy, or explaining what the seller's listing
agreement obligates (see *Where this stops*).

## Runs on

| Input | Where it comes from | Required? |
|---|---|---|
| Price point and property type | The listing conversation | Yes |
| Who the likely buyer is — first-time, move-up, cash, investor | Your read of your own market | Yes |
| Seller's urgency and cash position | Ask them | Yes |
| Whether the seller has already formed a view | The call | Yes |
| Your own recommendation | You — see step 5 | Yes |
| What you're seeing locally on compensation | [`../reference/your-practice/what-i-see-locally.md`](../reference/your-practice/what-i-see-locally.md) | Strongly |

**Not run on:** MLS data, local compensation statistics, or "what percentage of sellers in your area
offer." You do not have those and will not estimate them. See
[`../reference/no-listing-data.md`](../reference/no-listing-data.md).

## The two questions

1. **What's the price point, and who's the realistic buyer for this house?**
2. **Has the seller already said something about it — and how tight is their cash at closing?**

## Method

**1. Kill the binary.** Sellers arrive thinking the question is *pay or don't pay*. It isn't. There are
at least four live positions, and naming them is most of the value you add:

| Position | What it means in practice | Fits when |
|---|---|---|
| **Offer up front** | You communicate a compensation offer to buyer-side agents as part of how the home is marketed — off-MLS, since offers of compensation can't be published there since August 2024 | Broad-market home, buyer pool is cash-tight, seller wants maximum reach |
| **Offer nothing, stay open** | No advertised offer; treat it as negotiable when an offer comes in | Strong market, distinctive property, seller wants to preserve every dollar and can absorb a slower start |
| **Case-by-case at offer stage** | Nothing signalled; each offer's compensation ask gets evaluated with the rest of its terms | Seller is analytical, or genuinely undecided — this is often the honest answer |
| **Flat amount, not a percentage** | A fixed dollar figure rather than a rate | High price point, where a percentage is a large number relative to the buyer-side work |

**2. Reframe the money.** The single most useful move in this conversation. Sellers experience buyer-agent
compensation as *paying the other side's help*, which feels like a defection. It isn't — it's the same
category of decision as a closing-cost concession or a rate buydown:

> **"It's not really 'paying their agent.' It's the same question as a closing-cost credit or a rate
> buydown: are you willing to use some of your proceeds to widen the pool of buyers who can actually
> write on this house? Sometimes yes, sometimes no. It depends who your buyer is."**

That reframe moves the conversation from loyalty to arithmetic, which is where the seller can actually
think.

**3. Work the buyer-pool question honestly — both directions.** This is where agents either add real
insight or slide into scare tactics. The mechanism is specific and it has limits:

- A buyer working under a written agreement has agreed to pay their agent some amount.
- If the seller side covers none of it, that buyer covers it themselves — in cash, at closing, on top
  of a down payment and closing costs.
- For a cash-tight buyer, that's a real constraint, and it's most binding at the entry level and on
  buyers already stretched. That's the pool effect, and it's genuine.
- **But it's a margin effect, not a wall.** Motivated buyers find a way, agents adjust their asks,
  and the ask can be handled at offer stage. An agent who tells a seller "no one will show your house"
  is overselling and the seller will discover it.

Prep the honest version:

> **"Realistically, some share of your buyer pool is cash-tight enough that this matters to them — most
> likely at the entry level. It's not a wall, it's a headwind, and you can decide to take it. What I
> don't want is for you to be surprised by the question when an offer comes in."**

**4. Ask, don't assume, the seller's constraint.** Four questions that decide it, and the agent should
walk in with them:

1. How tight is your cash at closing — is every dollar of proceeds already committed?
2. How fast do you need to be out?
3. If it costs you two extra weeks on market to keep that money, is that a trade you'd take?
4. Would you rather decide this now, or decide it when a real offer is in front of you?

Question 4 gives most sellers relief. Many of them don't need to decide today, and telling them so is
better service than pushing to a decision they'll revisit anyway.

**5. Bring a recommendation.** This is the part agents skip. "It's up to you" is technically true and
professionally empty — the seller hired judgment. Form a view, state it as a view, and leave the
decision where it belongs:

> **"My recommendation is [X], and here's why: [one reason tied to this house and this buyer pool].
> It's your call and I'll run it either way — but you asked what I'd do, and that's what I'd do."**

If the agent genuinely has no view, the missing input is usually step 1 of *Runs on* — they haven't
formed a picture of who the buyer is.

**6. Set the revisit.** Whatever the seller decides, the decision has a second moment: when an offer
arrives with a compensation ask attached. Agree now on what happens then, so it isn't a fresh crisis
in the middle of a negotiation.

## Output format

Produce exactly this. One screen.

---

**SELLER COMPENSATION PREP — [property/seller handle], [date]**
*Internal. Not for the client.*

**The house and the likely buyer**
Two lines. Price point, type, and who realistically writes on it — from the agent's own read.

**The reframe**
The proceeds-not-loyalty line, in quotes, adapted to this seller.

**Four positions, narrowed**
The menu from step 1, cut to the two or three that plausibly fit this listing. One line each on the
trade.

**Your recommendation**
Which one, one reason tied to this house, and the sentence that delivers it as a view rather than a
verdict — in quotes. If the agent hasn't formed one: `[NO VIEW YET — who's the buyer for this house?]`

**The four questions for the seller**
Adapted to this seller's situation.

**The pool effect, said honestly**
Two lines, in quotes, including the limit. Never the scare version.

**The revisit**
What happens when an offer arrives with a compensation ask. One line.

**Where this stops**
One line, per the section below.

## Where this stops

**Two stops on this job, and the second one is easy to miss.**

**Stop 1 — the document.** Recommending a business position is listing-agent work and squarely yours.
Explaining the listing agreement is not.

| Fine | Over the line |
|---|---|
| "My recommendation is to offer, and here's why." | "Your listing agreement obligates you to…" |
| "We'd handle it as part of the offer terms." | "Once you offer it, you're legally bound to…" |
| "You can revisit this when an offer comes in." | "This clause lets you withdraw it because…" |

If the seller asks whether an offer of compensation creates an enforceable obligation, or what their
listing agreement commits them to, that's counsel:

> **"That's a legal question and I'd be out of my depth answering it — genuinely. Your attorney can
> tell you what the document commits you to. What I can tell you is how I'd handle it in the deal."**

**Stop 2 — no "standard," no "customary," no "everyone."** Compensation is negotiable and always has
been; representing it as fixed, standard, or set by anyone is precisely what the litigation behind the
2024 practice changes was about. Never produce, and never let an agent say:

- "The standard is 2.5%."
- "That's just what everyone offers around here."
- "You have to offer something competitive or you're out of the market."

Instead: *"There's no standard — it's negotiable, and it always was. Here's how I'd think about it for
this house."* If the agent asks you what the going local rate is, refuse under
[`../rules.md`](../rules.md) Rule 1: you do not have local data and will not invent it. The national
aggregates in [`../reference/compensation-landscape.md`](../reference/compensation-landscape.md) are
dated context, not a local answer, and never a "standard."

**Your state, and what it doesn't change.** Whether a seller here would normally have an attorney on
the listing agreement, and what you may say about its compensation provisions, both vary — your broker
first, then a real-estate attorney in your state. That answer settles what you may say to the seller.
It does not change what this file produces: a recommendation you can defend, never a reading of the
document, and never a page addressed to the seller. See
[`../reference/the-upl-line.md`](../reference/the-upl-line.md) § *Your state's answer, and what it
doesn't change.*

## If you're missing something

- **No view of the buyer** → ask before producing. Every position in step 1 depends on it.
- **Seller already decided** → don't re-litigate. Prep the revisit and the offer-stage handling instead.
- **Agent wants a local percentage** → refuse per Rule 1 and redirect to
  `your-practice/what-i-see-locally.md`, which is where their own observations belong.
- **`what-i-see-locally.md` still placeholdered** → produce the framework, label it framework-only, and
  point at the file. Their own observation is the only local source this repo will ever have.
