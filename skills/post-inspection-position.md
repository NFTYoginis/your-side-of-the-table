# Post-inspection position

> **Internal — agent-facing prep. Not a client deliverable.**

How you think about a repair request before anyone drafts anything. This job ends where the paperwork
begins, and it ends there hard.

## When to use

- An inspection came back with a long list and your buyer is spiralling
- You're listing-side and a repair request just landed
- Your client wants to ask for everything and you know that's a mistake
- You need to decide between repair, credit, price reduction, and letting it go

**Not for:** drafting the request, drafting the response, or telling anyone what the inspection
contingency lets them do. See *Where this stops.*

## Runs on

| Input | Where it comes from | Required? |
|---|---|---|
| The inspection findings | Your client's report — they own it | Yes |
| Which side you're on | You | Yes |
| Where you are in the timeline | You | Yes |
| Your read on the market for this house | Your own experience | Yes |
| What the client actually wants — the house, or an exit | Ask them. This changes everything | Yes |
| Seller's situation, if known | You | Helpful |

**Not run on:** listing data, comps, repair-cost databases, or contractor pricing. You do not estimate
what a repair costs. Contractors do that, and the number in a real bid is the only one worth asking on.

## The two questions

1. **Which side are you on, and does your client want this house or want out?**
2. **What are the two or three findings that actually matter — and has anyone got a real bid on them?**

## Method

**1. Sort the findings before anything else.** Every inspection report produces a long list, and every
long list contains three different kinds of thing that need three different responses.

| Category | What it looks like | Default posture |
|---|---|---|
| **Material** | Safety, structural, systems at or past end of life, active water, anything that shows up in a lender or insurance conversation | This is what you ask on |
| **Deferred maintenance** | Real, but the kind of thing every house of this age has. Aging but functional | Usually absorbed. Asking on it is what makes a list read as remorse |
| **Cosmetic and inspector-boilerplate** | Recommendations, "monitor," "consult a specialist" on everything | Not an ask. Ever |

The single most common buyer-side error is treating the report's length as the size of the problem.
Inspectors are paid to write down everything and are professionally obliged to over-flag. A 40-item
report on a 1974 house is a normal 1974 house.

**2. Decide what the ask is buying.** A repair request spends something. Name it before you write one:

- **A short, material list** reads as a reasonable buyer protecting themselves. It usually gets a
  response.
- **A long list** reads as a buyer looking for a discount or an exit, and sellers respond to that read,
  not to the items. It hardens the other side and it is the most common way a deal that was fine
  becomes a fight.

Three items is a request. Nineteen is a renegotiation, and everyone on the other side knows it.

**3. Pick the instrument.** Four available, and they are not interchangeable:

| Instrument | Who controls the work | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Repair before closing** | Seller — which usually means the cheapest bid that closes the item | Quality. A seller repairing under time pressure buys the minimum. Worth it for licensed/permitted work; poor for anything where craftsmanship matters |
| **Credit at closing** | Buyer, after the fact | Loan-program limits on seller-paid costs exist and vary. **Your lender tells you the cap, not me and not the inspection report.** Confirm before you ask for a number |
| **Price reduction** | Neither — it just changes the number | Interacts with appraisal and with the buyer's cash-to-close differently than a credit does. Different math, not a synonym |
| **Accept and proceed** | Nobody | A real option, and the right one more often than buyers believe. Especially strong when leverage is low |

If the buyer wants control of the work quality, that's a credit conversation. If they want the problem
gone before they own it, that's a repair conversation. Deciding which they want takes one question and
saves a week.

**4. Read the leverage honestly, out loud.** Buyer-side agents routinely overstate leverage to keep a
nervous client calm, and it produces asks that get rejected and clients who feel misled twice.

Leverage is high when: the house has sat, the seller has bought elsewhere, the finding is material and
would resurface with the next buyer, or financing/insurance are implicated.

Leverage is low when: there were multiple offers two weeks ago, the finding is age-appropriate, the
seller has options, or the buyer has already told everyone they're moving.

Say the honest version:

> **"We can ask. I want you to know going in that our leverage here is thin — the house had activity
> and this is the kind of finding a seller expects a buyer to absorb. If it's a no, are you still
> buying the house? That's the real question, and I'd rather know your answer before we send anything."**

**5. Listing-side: the mirror.** Sort the request the same way, then decide which of the four you're
countering with — and price the walk. A seller who refuses everything on a material finding is often
buying a re-list with a disclosure obligation attached, which is a worse outcome than the credit.
Frame it as a comparison, not as capitulation:

> **"You can say no. If they walk, the next buyer inspects too, and now you know what the report says.
> So the question isn't whether to spend the money — it's whether to spend it now with a buyer in hand."**

**6. Hand the decision back.** The client decides. Your job is to make the tradeoff legible — leverage,
instrument, and what each choice costs — and then to run whichever one they pick without sulking about it.

## Output format

Produce exactly this. One screen.

---

**POST-INSPECTION POSITION — [property handle], [buyer/listing side], [date]**
*Internal. Not for the client.*

**The sort**
Three short lists: material / deferred / cosmetic. Findings placed by the agent's own read of the
report. Never more than five in the material column — if there are more, say the deal has a bigger
question than a repair request.

**What you're asking for, and why that**
The instrument, one reason. If it's a credit: `— confirm the cap with the lender before naming a number.`

**Your leverage, honestly**
Two lines. High or thin, and what makes it so.

**The question for your client**
The one that has to be answered before anything goes out — usually "if this is a no, are you still
buying?" — in quotes.

**If it comes back no**
Two lines. What the fallback is, and whether it's worth taking.

**Where this stops**
One line, per the section below.

## Where this stops

**You prep the agent's thinking. The moment it becomes paper, it's not yours.**

This job sits closer to the line than any other in this repo, because the natural next sentence after
"here's what we should ask for" is "here's how to write it." That sentence is the practice of law.

| Fine | Over the line |
|---|---|
| "Ask for a credit on the panel, not a repair." | "Here's wording for the addendum." |
| "Confirm the cap with your lender." | "Under your contract you can request up to…" |
| "If it's a no, is she still buying?" | "You have the right to terminate under the inspection contingency." |
| "That's a question for your attorney." | "You'd get your earnest money back because…" |

Two refusals to have ready, because these are the two that will be asked:

**"Draft the repair request / addendum / response."**

> **"I don't write the paperwork — not a draft, not a starting point, not sample language. Your
> brokerage has the form and your broker has the training on it. What I'll do is make sure you know
> exactly what you're asking for and why before you fill it in."**

**"Can my buyer walk and keep their earnest money?"**

> **"That's a legal question about your contract and I'm not going to answer it, because the answer
> depends on the document and on your state — and because whatever I said, you'd repeat it to your
> client. Your broker first, an attorney if it's actually contested."**

Earnest money disposition, contingency rights, and termination consequences are contract
interpretation with money attached. They are the clearest examples in this repo of what a licence does
not cover. See [`../reference/the-upl-line.md`](../reference/the-upl-line.md).

## If you're missing something

- **No real bid on a material item** → say so and stop short of a number. An ask built on an internet
  estimate gets countered with a real bid and loses.
- **Client hasn't said whether they want the house** → get that answer before producing anything else.
  Every recommendation in this job flips on it.
- **The report is long and everything reads material to the agent** → that's a signal to slow down, not
  a signal to ask for everything. Work the sort together, one item at a time.
