# The fee conversation

> **Internal — agent-facing prep. Not a client deliverable.**

Saying your number, holding it, and answering the pushback it invites.

## When to use

- You have to state a fee to a specific client this week
- You've been quoting a number and then talking past it for ninety seconds
- You're deciding between percentage and flat fee — for this client or as a policy
- A client has already pushed back and you need the second answer, not the first
- You keep discounting and can't tell whether it's strategy or flinch

**Not for:** deciding what your fee should be. That's a business decision you make; this prepares you
to *say* it. If you don't have a number, that's the finding, and it comes out in the first exchange.

## The two questions

If the agent gives you nothing but "help me with my fee conversation":

1. **What's your number, and is it a percentage or a flat fee?**
2. **Has the client already pushed back — and if so, what exactly did they say?**

## Method

**1. Diagnose which conversation this actually is.** Four different problems wear the same sentence.

| The real problem | Tell | What fixes it |
|---|---|---|
| **The statement** | The agent talks for 90 seconds after the number | Step 2 — structure and the pause |
| **The structure** | "Should I go flat on this one?" | Step 3 — percentage vs flat |
| **The pushback** | A specific objection already landed | Step 4 + [`../reference/objection-library.md`](../reference/objection-library.md) |
| **The number** | Agent doesn't have one, or has three | Not a conversation problem. Say so — see *If you're missing something* |

**2. Fix the statement first — it's three moves, and most agents run them in the wrong order.**

The failure mode is universal and it's not confidence, it's sequence. Agents *justify, then state, then
keep justifying* — which frames the number as something requiring defense before anyone has challenged it.

The order that holds:

1. **State it.** Flat, complete sentence, no run-up. *"My fee is 2.5% of the purchase price."*
   No "typically," no "around," no "what I usually try to get."
2. **Stop talking.** Two seconds of silence. This is the whole technique and it's the part agents
   can't do. The pause signals the number is settled. Filling it signals it isn't.
3. **Then what it buys** — but only after the pause, and in specifics, not adjectives.

Prep the agent to expect that the client's first response to the pause is often nothing at all, and
that nothing is a good outcome.

**3. Percentage vs flat — the real tradeoffs, not the marketing ones.**

Neither is correct. They fail differently, and which failure the agent can live with is the decision.

| | **Percentage** | **Flat fee** |
|---|---|---|
| **Reads as** | Standard. Invisible because it's expected | A choice, which invites the question "how did you land there?" |
| **On a low-price home** | Underpays badly — the work is nearly identical to a high-price deal | Protects you. Often the better structure on the bottom third of your market |
| **On a high-price home** | Pays well; also the place clients push hardest | Leaves money on the table, and clients notice a bargain and trust it less |
| **Buyer-side alignment problem** | A percentage of purchase price means you earn more the more they spend. A sharp buyer will notice. Have the answer ready | Clean. Your pay doesn't move with their price — this is genuinely the strongest argument for flat on the buyer side |
| **Listing-side alignment** | Aligned — higher sale price pays you more, which is what the seller wants | Weakens the alignment story you'd otherwise get for free |
| **Negotiation surface** | One number, easy to shave a quarter point off | One number, and shaving it looks arbitrary, which is protective |
| **The hard part** | Defending the rate | Defending the derivation |

If the agent is buyer-side and keeps getting hit with *"so you make more if I spend more?"* — that's
not an objection to their rate, it's an objection to percentage as a structure, and no amount of
value language answers a structural complaint. Flat fee answers it. That's the actual decision.

**4. Prep the specific pushback, not a general defense.** Pull the matching entry from
[`../reference/objection-library.md`](../reference/objection-library.md) and adapt it to this client's
words. Two rules for every objection response:

- **Concede what's true first.** "You're right that some of this is more visible than it used to be"
  costs nothing and buys the rest of the sentence.
- **Answer, then re-state the number.** An objection response that trails off without returning to the
  fee has conceded it. End on the number.

**5. Set the floor before the conversation, not during it.** Ask the agent: *below what number do you
decline this client?* An agent who hasn't decided will discover their floor mid-conversation, which
means they don't have one. If `your-practice/fee-position.md` has no floor line, say so — that's a
30-second decision that changes how the whole conversation feels.

**6. Separate the fee from where the money comes from.** On the buyer side post-2024 these are two
different conversations, and collapsing them is what makes agents sound evasive.

- **Your fee** is what you charge. It's yours to state.
- **The source** is what you'll do about getting it covered — what you'll ask the listing side for,
  and what you'll do if they cover less.

Describe your own approach: *"My fee is 2.5%. In practice, I'll ask the seller's side to cover it as
part of the offer, and most of the time they do. If they come back with less, we talk about the gap
before we write anything."* That is your business practice, and it is entirely yours to describe.

What you must not do is describe what the agreement obligates the buyer to pay. Same words, different
speaker — see *Where this stops*.

## Output format

Produce exactly this. One screen.

---

**FEE PREP — [client handle], [buyer/listing side], [date]**
*Internal. Not for the client.*

**Which conversation this is**
One line, from the four in step 1.

**Your line**
The statement, in quotes, exactly as they'd say it. Then the instruction: `— stop here. Two seconds.`
Then the "what it buys" follow-on in specifics, drawn from what the agent told you.

**Structure note** *(only if percentage-vs-flat is live)*
Which structure fits this client and this price point, in two or three lines, with the failure the
agent is choosing to accept.

**The pushback and your answer**
The objection in the client's own words if you have them, otherwise the most likely one. Then the
response, in quotes, built as: concede → answer → return to the number.

**Your floor**
The number, from file. If not on file: `[NOT SET — decide this before the meeting, not during it]`.

**If they hold out**
Two lines: what you'd offer that isn't a rate cut, and the sentence that walks away cleanly.

**Where this stops**
One line, per the section below.

## Where this stops

**You prep the agent's fee — their number, their words, their business. You never touch what the
paperwork does with it.**

| Fine | Over the line |
|---|---|
| "My fee is 2.5%." | "The agreement says you owe 2.5% if…" |
| "I'll ask the seller's side to cover it." | "This clause means the seller has to pay…" |
| "If they cover less, we'll talk about the gap." | "You'd be liable for the difference under…" |
| "Read it before you sign." | "It basically just says…" |

The distinction is not a technicality. *"If they cover less, we'll talk about the gap"* describes what
the agent will do. *"You'd be liable for the difference"* states a legal consequence of a contract — the
unauthorized practice of law in all fifty states, and in 2026 quite possibly uninsured, since E&O
carriers began adding generative-AI exclusions. See [`../reference/the-upl-line.md`](../reference/the-upl-line.md).

You also never tell an agent **what to charge.** If asked directly:

> **"Not my call, and you wouldn't want it to be — I don't know your market, your costs, or your
> pipeline. What I can do is help you say the number you've decided on, and pressure-test whether
> you've actually decided."**

## If you're missing something

- **No fee on file** → this job cannot run on a guess. Ask once, plainly: *"What's your number right
  now — the one you'd say out loud today?"* Then have them write it into `your-practice/fee-position.md`.
  Never produce a fee brief around an invented rate.
- **Three different fees depending on mood** → that's the real problem and it's worth naming. An agent
  who quotes differently by client will be inconsistent under pressure, and clients talk.
- **No floor** → produce the rest, mark the floor `[NOT SET]`, and flag it as the thing to decide first.
- **No specifics about what they do** → mark `[NEEDS YOUR SPECIFIC]`. Do not write them a value
  proposition out of adjectives; a fee defended with adjectives is a fee that gets cut.
