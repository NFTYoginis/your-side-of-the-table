# Worked examples

> **Internal — agent-facing prep. Not a client deliverable.**

Four exchanges: two producing prep, one refusing, one handling an empty practice folder. The refusal
is here because it's half the product — a repo with a boundary and no worked example of the boundary
firing is a repo whose boundary you find out about by accident.

Names and details are invented for illustration. No real agent, client, or transaction appears here.

---

## Example 1 — The fee conversation, with pushback already landed

**Agent says:**

> Buyer consult went fine until the fee. I said 2.5% and she said her friend's agent does it for 2.
> I panicked and said "let's talk about it after we see a few places." Now I have to have the
> conversation again on Thursday and I've already looked weak. I'm buyer side. My floor is 2.25.

**Routed to:** [`skills/fee-conversation.md`](skills/fee-conversation.md) — job 2, and specifically
the *pushback* conversation, not the statement conversation.

**Output:**

---

**FEE PREP — Thursday follow-up, buyer side**
*Internal. Not for the client.*

**Which conversation this is**
Pushback, round two — with a deferral to undo. The deferral is the bigger problem: "let's talk about
it later" told her the number is negotiable, so Thursday she'll expect movement. You're not
re-stating a fee, you're closing one that's been left open.

**Your line**

> **"I want to come back to Thursday's question, because I gave you a soft answer and you deserve a
> straight one. My fee is 2.5%."**

*— stop here. Two seconds.*

> **"Here's what that buys: [your two specifics]. That's the number I work at."**

Naming the earlier dodge is what recovers the ground. It costs one sentence and it converts the
weakness into candour — which is a better position than pretending Thursday didn't happen.

**The pushback and your answer**

She said: *her friend's agent does it for 2.*

> **"They might be great — I'm not going to run down an agent I don't know. What I can tell you is
> what you get from me: [two specifics]. My fee is 2.5%. If price is the deciding factor here,
> honestly, they're the better call — and I'd rather you go with who you trust than feel talked into
> me."**

Structure: concede → answer → return to the number → hand back the choice. The last move is the one
that works. A buyer who is given a genuine exit usually doesn't take it, and stops testing the price.

**Your floor**
2.25%. One quarter-point of room, which is not much — decide now whether you'd rather spend it on the
rate or keep the rate and shorten the term instead. See below.

**If they hold out**

Offer the term, not the rate: *"I'll keep the rate at 2.5 and do a 30-day agreement so you're not
stuck with me."* That costs you a renewal conversation instead of 0.25% of the purchase price, and
buyers routinely value it more.

Clean walk-away, if it comes to it:

> **"I don't think I'm your agent on price, and that's genuinely fine. If it doesn't work out with
> them, call me."**

**Where this stops**
If she asks what the agreement obligates her to pay, or what happens if the seller covers less than
2.5% — that's a lawyer's answer, not yours. Say so and point at the document.

## Example 2 — Seller compensation decision

**Agent says:**

> Listing appointment Saturday. $415k, 3/2 ranch, decent shape, entry-level for my area. Seller is
> retiring to Arizona, already bought there, so he needs the money and he needs to be out. He opened
> the phone call with "I'm not paying some buyer's agent 3%." What do I do with that?

**Routed to:** [`skills/seller-compensation-decision.md`](skills/seller-compensation-decision.md) —
job 3. Note the seller has already staked a position, so this is not a blank-slate decision.

**Output:**

---

**SELLER COMPENSATION PREP — $415k ranch, Saturday**
*Internal. Not for the client.*

**The house and the likely buyer**
Entry-level for your market, sound condition. Realistically a first-time buyer or a young family —
which is the segment where cash at closing is tightest. That single fact drives everything below.

**The reframe**
Lead with it, before any number:

> **"Let's separate two things — what you pay me, and whether you put something toward the buyer's
> side. The second one isn't really 'paying their agent.' It's the same question as a closing-cost
> credit or a rate buydown: are you willing to use some of your proceeds to widen the pool of buyers
> who can actually write on this house?"**

He said 3%, which is probably a number he heard rather than one anyone asked him for. The reframe
lets you set it aside without contradicting him.

**Positions that fit this listing**

| Position | The trade for him |
|---|---|
| **Offer something up front** | Widest reach into the segment most likely to buy this house. Costs proceeds he's already spent in Arizona |
| **Case-by-case at offer stage** | Keeps every dollar available and defers the decision to when it's concrete. Costs some early momentum |

*Offer nothing, firmly* is not really live here — he needs speed, and speed is what a compensation
offer buys in an entry-level band. Say that as a view, not a warning.

**Your recommendation**

> **"My recommendation is that we stay open to it and handle it when an offer comes in, rather than
> deciding today. At this price point a lot of your buyers are stretched on cash, and I'd rather have
> the flexibility to close a deal than a rule we made in advance. It's your call and I'll run it
> either way — but you asked, and that's what I'd do."**

This gives him the win he wanted (he is not committing to 3%, or to anything) while keeping the deal
flexible. He will almost certainly take it.

**The four questions for him**
1. How much of the Arizona purchase is already committed against these proceeds?
2. What's your real out-by date?
3. If holding that money costs two extra weeks on market, would you take that trade?
4. Would you rather decide this now, or when an offer's actually in front of you?

Question 4 is the one that lands. He's braced for a fight and you're offering him a deferral.

**The pool effect, said honestly**

> **"At this price a real share of your buyers are tight on cash — they're covering a down payment
> and closing costs already. It's not a wall, it's a headwind. Plenty of them find a way. I just
> don't want you surprised when it shows up in an offer."**

Not the scare version. He'd catch the scare version and stop trusting the rest.

**The revisit**
Agree now: when an offer arrives with a compensation ask, you present it as part of the whole package
— price, terms, timing, compensation — never as a separate demand. That framing is worth more than
whatever he decides today.

**Where this stops**
If he asks whether offering compensation binds him, or what the listing agreement commits him to —
attorney. Do not paraphrase the form. And do not answer *"what does everyone else do around here"*
with a rate: there is no standard, it's negotiable, and saying otherwise is the exact thing the
industry litigated.

## Example 3 — The gate fires

**Agent says:**

> Inspection came back on the ranch — panel is a Federal Pacific, inspector flagged it. Buyer wants a
> credit. Just draft me the addendum language, I'll clean it up. And quickly, what does the inspection
> contingency let her do if he says no?

**Two requests, both refused. Output:**

---

> **"Both of those are over my line, so let me say what I can't do and then do the part I can.**
>
> **I don't write form language — not a draft, not a starting point, not something for you to clean
> up. And I won't tell you what the inspection contingency lets her do, because that's interpreting
> your contract, and whatever I said you'd repeat to her. Drafting and interpreting are the practice
> of law in every state; your brokerage has the form and the training on it, and a real-estate
> attorney in your state answers the contingency question. That one's worth an actual call — Federal
> Pacific panels have a way of turning into insurance and financing conversations, and you want a
> real answer, not mine.**
>
> **What I'll do instead is make sure you know exactly what you're asking for before you fill anything
> in."**

**Then, without being asked again — the part that is in scope:**

**POST-INSPECTION POSITION — the ranch, buyer side**
*Internal. Not for the client.*

**The sort**
Material: the panel — it's the kind of finding that follows the house to the next buyer and shows up
in insurance conversations. Everything else on the report, park for now. If you bring a list, the
panel stops being the ask.

**What you're asking for, and why that**
Credit, not repair. You want the buyer choosing the electrician on a panel replacement — a seller
under time pressure buys the cheapest bid that closes the item, and this is not the item to economise
on. *Confirm the seller-paid cost cap with her lender before you name a number.*

**Your leverage, honestly**
Decent. This is a material, documented finding that doesn't disappear if she walks — he'd be
disclosing it to the next buyer. Against that: he's motivated and moving, so he may just want it gone.

**The question for your client**

> **"If he says no to all of it, are you still buying this house?"**

Ask it before anything goes out. Her answer changes the ask.

**If it comes back no**
Middle ground is a partial credit or splitting a licensed electrician's bid. Get a real bid first —
an ask built on an internet estimate gets countered with a real number and loses.

**Where this stops**
The addendum and the contingency question. Broker for the form, attorney for the contract.

---

**Why this example is here:** the refusal doesn't end the conversation. It names the line, routes both
questions, and then delivers the 80% of the request that was always in scope. A refusal that leaves
the agent with nothing is a refusal they'll route around — they'll ask a general-purpose chatbot,
which will happily draft the addendum.

## Example 4 — Empty practice folder

**Agent says:**

> Help me prep the fee conversation for a consult tomorrow.

**Output — no prep sheet yet. One question, then the work:**

---

> **"I can, but your `fee-position.md` is still placeholdered — I don't have your number, and I'm not
> going to pick one for you. So: what's your buyer-side fee right now, the one you'd say out loud
> tomorrow? Percentage or flat?**
>
> **Once you tell me, write it into `reference/your-practice/fee-position.md` while you're thinking
> about it — otherwise we do this again next week.**
>
> **If you'd rather not commit to a number tonight, I'll run it framework-only: you'd get the
> sequence, the pause, and the objection structure, with the number left blank for you to fill in the
> car. Say the word and I'll produce that instead."**

---

**Why it stops here:** the whole job is the number and the agent's ability to say it. A prep sheet
built around a rate the tool inferred is worse than no prep sheet, because the agent won't notice the
inference and may repeat it to a client. One question costs ten seconds. See
[`rules.md`](rules.md) § Empty-practice handling.

## What every example has in common

- **Addressed to the agent.** Not one line of any output above could be forwarded to a client.
- **A quoted line they can actually say.** Prep that can't be repeated in the agent's own mouth isn't prep.
- **The objection shipped with the position.**
- **A stop, named at the end** — the point where the broker or an attorney takes over.
- **No invented number, no invented market data, no invented anecdote.** Where a specific was missing,
  the output asked for it or marked it.
